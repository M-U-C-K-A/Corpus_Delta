# Pollinator Decline: When Agriculture Loses Its Allies

Pollinators—bees, butterflies, hoverflies, and other insects—are essential to the reproduction of 75% of global food crops. Their widespread decline poses a direct threat to food security.

## The Scale of Decline

In Europe, more than **40% of wild bee species** are in decline. In the United States, beekeepers lose 30-40% of their colonies each year. Long-term studies show a 75% decline in flying insect biomass in Germany since 1989.

## Causes of Decline

**Pesticides**: Neonicotinoids, in particular, affect the bees' nervous system, disrupting their orientation, foraging ability, and reproduction. The effect is often sublethal: bees don't die immediately but weaken.

**Habitat loss**: Intensive agriculture has eliminated hedgerows, flower meadows, and field margins where pollinators found food and shelter.

**Parasites and diseases**: Varroa destructor decimates honeybee colonies. Globalization spreads these pathogens.

**Climate change**: Desynchronization between plant flowering and pollinator emergence. Expansion of parasite ranges.

## Economic Consequences

The economic value of pollination is estimated at more than **$150 billion per year** globally. Without pollinators, yields of many crops (fruits, vegetables, oilseeds) would collapse.

## Solutions

Drastic pesticide reduction, conversion to organic agriculture, creation of ecological corridors, flower strips along field edges, pollinator-friendly gardens and green spaces, and banning the most harmful neonicotinoids.
