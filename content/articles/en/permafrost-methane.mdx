# Permafrost Thawing: The Positive Feedback Loop

Permafrost refers to ground that remains completely frozen (0°C or colder) for at least two years straight. It covers about 24% of the land in the Northern Hemisphere and holds massive amounts of frozen organic carbon.

## Structure and Dynamics

![Permafrost Cross Section](/articles/permafrost-diagram.png)

Permafrost consists of two main layers:
1.  **The Active Layer**: The surface layer that thaws in summer and refreezes in winter. This is where vegetation grows.
2.  **The Permafrost Table**: The deep layer that remains permanently frozen.

With global warming, the active layer is thickening, eating into the deep permafrost.

## Greenhouse Gas Release

Thawing exposes ancient organic matter (remains of plants and animals) to microbial activity.
*   **With Oxygen (Aerobic)**: Microbes decompose organic matter into CO₂.
*   **Without Oxygen (Anaerobic)**: In water-saturated soils (peatlands, thaw lakes), decomposition produces methane (CH₄).

Since methane is a very potent greenhouse gas, its release accelerates warming, which accelerates thawing, creating a dangerous **positive feedback loop**.

## Thermokarst and Soil Instability

The melting of ground ice leads to soil collapse, forming irregular landscapes called thermokarst. This threatens infrastructure (roads, buildings, pipelines) in Arctic regions and alters local hydrology, creating new lakes that are often intense sources of methane.

## Tipping Point?

Scientists fear that massive permafrost thaw could become irreversible past a certain temperature threshold (tipping point), releasing hundreds of gigatons of carbon and making the Paris Agreement goals impossible to reach.
