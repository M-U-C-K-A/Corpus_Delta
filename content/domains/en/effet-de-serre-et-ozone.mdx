These are two different subjects, and confusing them prevents understanding either. The
greenhouse effect plays out in the first ten kilometres of the atmosphere and concerns the
infrared radiation the Earth emits. The ozone hole plays out around thirty kilometres up
and concerns the ultraviolet the Sun sends. The gases involved are not the same, the
molecules do not do the same thing, and only one of the two problems is on its way to
being solved.

## What a greenhouse gas actually does

<Mechanism name="effet-de-serre" />

The atmosphere is not a blanket. A blanket slows convection; a
<Term id="gaz-a-effet-de-serre">greenhouse gas</Term> does something else entirely, and the
mechanism comes in three steps.

Sunlight is essentially visible and near-infrared radiation. Nitrogen and oxygen
molecules, which make up 99 % of the air, are transparent to it: the light reaches the
ground almost unobstructed. The ground warms and re-emits, but at a completely different
wavelength — thermal infrared, because a body at 15 °C radiates around 10 micrometres.

And there, the atmosphere stops being transparent. Molecules with three atoms or more —
CO₂, H₂O, CH₄, N₂O — have vibration modes whose frequencies fall precisely in that range.
They absorb this radiation, then re-emit it in every direction. Half goes back down.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why nitrogen and oxygen take no part">
A molecule absorbs an infrared photon only if its vibration changes its dipole moment. N₂
and O₂, made of two identical atoms, stay perfectly symmetrical whatever their vibrations:
their dipole moment remains zero, and they are blind to infrared. It is for this reason of
pure molecular geometry that 1 % of the atmosphere governs the temperature of the other
99 %.
</Callout>

## The most solid series in the discipline

CO₂ concentration requires no reconstruction: it is measured directly, far from local
sources. The Mauna Loa series, begun in 1958, is the longest continuous measurement of the
atmosphere and the anchor point for everything else.

<Chart dataset="co2-mauna-loa" kind="area" theme="carbone" />

What the annual curve does not show is that the acceleration is itself under way: it is
not only the concentration that is rising, it is the speed at which it rises.

<DecadeBars dataset="co2-growth-rate" from={1960} theme="carbone" />

Polar ice cores and marine archives place these values in their long context: the current
concentration has no equivalent over the past two million years
<Cite id="rae-2021-atmospheric-over-past-million-years" />. The annual accounting of
fluxes — what is emitted, what the ocean and vegetation absorb, what stays airborne — is
kept by the global carbon budget
<Cite id="friedlingstein-2026-global-carbon-budget-2025" />.

## The other gases, and why they are not naively added up

<Indicators ids="methane-global,nitrous-oxide-global,sf6-global" decimals={1} drift="hausse-defavorable" columns={3} />

Methane is about eighty times more effective than CO₂ over twenty years, but it breaks
down in a dozen; nitrous oxide is less powerful but persists more than a century; sulphur
hexafluoride exceeds three thousand years. Reducing them to a
<Term id="equivalent-co2">CO₂ equivalent</Term> therefore requires choosing a time horizon,
and that choice changes the rankings. The hundred-year
<Term id="potentiel-de-rechauffement-global">global warming potential</Term>, the most
widely used, mechanically understates short-lived gases.

This asymmetry has a consequence for action: cutting methane affects temperature within a
decade, where cutting CO₂ only does so after a century. The two levers are not
interchangeable, and the first does not excuse skipping the second
<Cite id="shindell-2012-simultaneously-mitigating-near-term-climate" />.

The methane budget remains the hardest to close: natural and human sources are entangled —
wetlands, livestock, gas leaks, landfills — and inventories still diverge appreciably from
atmospheric measurements
<Cite id="saunois-2025-global-methane-budget-2000-2020" /><Cite id="kirschke-2013-three-decades-global-methane-sources" />.

## The ozone layer: another storey, another problem

<Mechanism name="couche-ozone" />

Thirty kilometres up, ozone absorbs solar ultraviolet. It is not stored there: it is
manufactured and destroyed there continuously, in an equilibrium sustained by the
radiation itself. What CFCs broke is that equilibrium.

The point to remember comes down to one word: **catalysis**. A chlorine atom released by
the photolysis of a CFC breaks an ozone molecule, then leaves the reaction intact and
starts again — tens of thousands of times before it leaves the stratosphere. It was not
the quantity of CFCs emitted that opened a hole above a continent, it was this reuse.

## The indicator that is coming back down

The Montreal Protocol, signed in 1987, banned these compounds. Thirty-five years later,
the halogen load of the stratosphere is decreasing. It is, to our knowledge, the only
indicator on this page heading in the right direction.

<Chart dataset="ozone-depleting-gas-index" kind="line" theme="politiques" />

The index is 100 at the late-1990s maximum and 0 at the 1980 level, taken as the reference
from before the hole. The two curves measure the same thing in two places: the air that
feeds the Antarctic vortex has spent longer at altitude, so its index declines several
years behind the mid-latitude one.

<Chart dataset="stratospheric-halogens-eesc" kind="area" theme="politiques" />

<Callout variant="caution" title="What this success does not prove">
It is often concluded that the climate will be settled the same way. The comparison has
its limits. CFCs were produced by a handful of manufacturers, for uses that had
substitutes, and represented a minuscule fraction of the world economy. Fossil fuels are
the energy of everything else. What the Montreal Protocol demonstrates is that a binding
international agreement can bend a global atmospheric curve — not that the next problem
will be of the same difficulty.
</Callout>

## One overlap, all the same

The two subjects do touch at one point. CFCs are also powerful greenhouse gases, and
banning them avoided appreciable additional warming. Conversely, their first-generation
substitutes, HFCs, do not destroy ozone but do warm — which required a separate amendment,
in 2016, to bring them under control in turn. The total radiative balance accounts for
these compounds, and that is why the forcing curve cannot be deduced from CO₂ alone
<Cite id="forster-2026-indicators-global-climate-change-2025" />.
