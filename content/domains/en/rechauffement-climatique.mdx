A planet that receives from the Sun exactly what it returns to space has a stable
temperature. The whole of global warming lies in the fact that this equality has been
broken for about a century and a half, by a little over one watt per square metre — a gap
of around half a percent between what comes in and what goes out.

The figure looks trivial. It is not, for a reason that is far from intuitive: it applies
permanently, over the entire surface of the globe, and what it accumulates does not go
away.

## Where the energy goes

<Mechanism name="stock-energie" />

The first surprise, when you follow that energy, is that it barely goes where we feel it.
Air is a poor heat reservoir: little is needed to warm it, and it holds no more than that.
The ocean, for equal volume, has a heat capacity roughly a thousand times greater — and it
is deep.

The consequence: <Term id="contenu-thermique-de-l-ocean">ocean heat content</Term> is the
least noisy measure of the imbalance. An El Niño year makes air temperature jump by a
tenth or two of a degree, then fall back; the ocean rises without a jolt, because it
integrates the imbalance rather than suffering it
<Cite id="johnson-2020-warming-trends-increasingly-dominate-global" />.

<Chart dataset="ocean-heat-content" kind="area" theme="chaleur" />

This is why a headline such as "warming has paused" has been written several times about
air temperature, and never about the ocean
<Cite id="cheng-2024-new-record-ocean-temperatures-related" />.

## A mean temperature that exists nowhere

The quantity everybody quotes — "+1.2 °C since the pre-industrial era" — is not a direct
measurement. No thermometer reads the mean temperature of the globe. What is built instead
is an <Term id="anomalie-de-temperature">anomaly</Term>: station temperatures are not
averaged, their departures from their own normal are.

That shift is not a technicality. Absolute temperatures depend on altitude, on distance
from the sea, on exposure; two stations ten kilometres apart can differ by several degrees
permanently. Their anomalies, on the other hand, resemble one another across hundreds of
kilometres, because a cold front cools everyone at once. That is what makes interpolation
between stations legitimate.

<Chart dataset="temperature-anomaly-gistemp" kind="area" theme="chaleur" />

Arranged by decade, the same series says something the annual curve drowns in its own
noise: no decade has been colder than the one before it since the 1960s.

## Land warms twice as fast

The global mean has one flaw: nobody lives in it. Two thirds of the Earth's surface is
ocean, which warms markedly more slowly than the continents — it spreads the heat it
receives through a thick layer, and devotes a good part of it to evaporation.

<Chart dataset="land-ocean-warming" kind="line" theme="chaleur" />

The gap between the two curves is one of the reasons the global figure sits consistently
below what people observe where they live. This is not a contradiction: it is the
difference between a surface average and an experience lived on dry land.

## What drives it: radiative forcing

<Term id="forcage-radiatif">Radiative forcing</Term> is the direct measure of the
imbalance imposed on the <Term id="bilan-radiatif">radiative balance</Term>, in watts per
square metre. NOAA publishes it every year for long-lived greenhouse gases, from measured
concentrations — not from a model.

<Chart dataset="greenhouse-forcing-aggi" kind="area" theme="carbone" />

<Callout variant="info" title="Why forcing alone does not predict the warming">
Between the forcing and the final temperature sit the
<Term id="retroaction-climatique">feedbacks</Term>: water vapour, which amplifies; clouds,
whose sign was long uncertain; the melting of bright surfaces. Their sum is the
<Term id="sensibilite-climatique">climate sensitivity</Term>, and it carries most of the
remaining uncertainty. Its estimate was narrowed considerably by combining three
independent families of constraints — process physics, the historical record, and
palaeoclimates <Cite id="sherwood-2020-assessment-earths-climate-sensitivity-using" />.
</Callout>

## What is already committed

One question keeps coming back: if all emissions stopped tomorrow, how much further would
the Earth warm? Intuition says "a lot", since the ocean has not finished absorbing. The
answer from models is more subtle: stopping emissions makes CO₂ concentration fall faster
than the ocean gives its heat back, and the two effects largely cancel. The additional
committed warming is on the order of a few hundredths of a degree, not a few tenths
<Cite id="macdougall-2020-there-warming-pipeline-multi-model" />.

That result has a direct political bearing, and it is one of the few firmly established
pieces of good news in the field: the level at which warming stops depends essentially on
what is emitted from now on, not on an inescapable inheritance.

## What projections say, and what they do not

End-of-century trajectories are not forecasts. They are answers to emission assumptions,
and the spread between them measures a social choice first, not a scientific uncertainty.
The constraint brought by the trend observed since 1970 narrows the high end appreciably,
without changing the fact that most of the dispersion comes from the chosen scenario
<Cite id="tokarska-2020-past-warming-trend-constrains-future" />.
