The tide is not a climate subject. It is set by celestial mechanics, it was here long
before us, and warming barely alters it. That is exactly what makes it useful: because it
does not change, it serves as a yardstick, and what does change can be read against it.

## Why two tides a day

<Mechanism name="maree-lunaire" />

Lunar attraction stretches the ocean into two bulges: one facing the Moon, where the pull
is strongest, the other opposite it, where the pull is weakest and the rotation of the
Earth–Moon system wins out. Both bulges stay aligned with the Moon.

The common misunderstanding is to picture a wave travelling around the globe. That is not
what happens: the bulges are roughly stationary relative to the Moon, and it is the Earth
that turns beneath them. Each coast therefore passes through two bulges a day, hence two
high waters — and the daily lag of about fifty minutes comes from the Moon having moved
along its orbit in the meantime.

The Sun produces the same effect, twice as weak. When the two line up, at new and full
moon, tides are strong: these are spring tides. In quadrature they work against each
other: these are neap tides.

## The 18.6-year cycle

The plane of the lunar orbit is tilted by about 5° from that of the Earth's orbit, and
that plane swings slowly: it returns to the same position every 18.6 years. This is the
**nodal cycle**, and it modulates the tidal range by a few percent.

A few percent on a range of one metre forty makes several centimetres — a signal that is
perfectly legible in a long series, and one of the few astronomical quantities a tide
gauge records directly.

<Chart dataset="tidal-range-battery" kind="line" theme="ocean" height={320} />

The New York series spans more than a century, that is five and a half cycles. The two
curves tell two superimposed stories: the range oscillates without drifting, the mean
level drifts without oscillating.

<Callout variant="info" title="What the oscillation makes it possible to check">
A tide gauge measures a water height, not a mean level: ground motion, atmospheric
pressure and currents still have to be corrected for. The nodal cycle, whose period is
known in advance to astronomical precision, offers a control: if an instrument does not
find it where it should be, it is the instrument or the processing that needs revisiting,
not the ocean.
</Callout>

## The threshold does not move, the sea rises

<Mechanism name="maregraphe" />

Here is where the tide stops being a matter of astronomy. A tide gauge counts the number
of days a year on which high water exceeds a flooding threshold fixed once and for all,
set from the height at which the water starts causing trouble — flooded streets, storm
drains backing up.

The tidal range has not changed. Neither has the threshold. What changes is the mean level
on which that tide is superimposed.

<Chart dataset="high-tide-flooding-battery" kind="bar" theme="risques" height={320} />

The result is not proportional, and that is the whole interest of this indicator. High
water heights are distributed around a mean; raising that mean by a few centimetres does
not shift the distribution by one notch, it tips above the threshold a whole population of
tides that used to pass just below it. A steady rise therefore produces growth that runs
away.

<DecadeBars dataset="high-tide-flooding-battery" from={1930} theme="risques" decimals={1} />

The annual count is very noisy — one storm season or one El Niño episode is enough to
double it. It is the decade average that carries the signal, and it is unambiguous.

## What this changes for the notion of risk

<Indicators ids="sea-level-altimetry,ocean-heat-content" decimals={1} drift="hausse-defavorable" columns={2} />

<Term id="submersion-marine">Coastal flooding</Term> is the textbook case of what
specialists call a compound event: it results not from a single cause but from the
coincidence of several — a spring tide, a depression lifting the water surface, an onshore
wind, sometimes a river flood arriving at the same time from upstream
<Cite id="zscheischler-2018-future-climate-risk-compound-events" />. Each of these factors
taken alone remains ordinary; it is their meeting that floods.

The rise in mean level creates none of these factors. It raises the common starting point
for all of them, which amounts to shortening the
<Term id="periode-de-retour">return period</Term> of every combination. A hundred-year
event becomes a ten-year one without any storm having changed its nature
<Cite id="jiang-2024-compounding-effects-flood-drivers-challenge" />.

## The mean level, and how we know it

<Chart dataset="sea-level-altimetry" kind="area" theme="ocean" />

Two independent measurement systems coexist. Tide gauges, the older of the two, give a
**relative** level: they are attached to ground that moves as well — subsiding deltas,
rebounding regions formerly under ice. Satellite altimetry, since 1993, gives an
**absolute** level, referenced to the centre of the Earth.

Their agreement over the common period is what grounds confidence in the reconstruction of
the previous century, made from tide gauges alone
<Cite id="church-2011-sea-level-rise-late-19th" />. The global rate is not constant: it has
more than doubled since the start of the altimetry series
<Cite id="hamlington-2024-rate-global-sea-level-rise" />.

<Callout variant="caution" title="One tide gauge is not the world">
The series on this page come from a single station, chosen for its length. The relative
level there combines the global rise with local ground motion, which differs from coast to
coast: some see a rise twice the average, others see a fall. A station illustrates a
mechanism; it does not measure the planet. Altimetry does that.
</Callout>
