"Drought" names at least four different phenomena, and confusing them leads to dead-end
arguments in which each side leans on a different indicator.

## Four droughts, four timescales

**Meteorological** drought is a shortfall in precipitation over a given period. It sets in
within weeks and can lift just as fast.

**Agricultural** drought concerns soil moisture in the root zone. It depends on rainfall,
but also on temperature, wind and air humidity, which govern evaporation. The same rainfall
deficit produces very different stress depending on whether it falls in cool weather or
during a <Term id="canicule">heatwave</Term>.

**Hydrological** drought affects river flows and groundwater levels. It responds with a lag
of several months and recovers slowly: heavy rain does not recharge an aquifer in one season.

**Socio-economic** drought arrives when the available resource no longer meets demand. It
depends as much on that demand as on availability, which makes it the only one of the four
where human organisation weighs directly.

## The role of evaporative demand

Warming does not act only on precipitation, whose evolution remains regionally contrasted
and hard to project. It acts above all on the **thirst of the atmosphere**: warmer air can
hold more water vapour and therefore draws more moisture from soils and vegetation, at
unchanged rainfall.

## Rarely an isolated hazard

Drought and heat sustain one another: dry soil no longer spends energy evaporating water,
and that energy then goes into heating the air. This loop makes drought-heat spells textbook
<Term id="evenement-compose">compound events</Term>, which risk analyses treating each hazard
separately underestimate
<Cite id="zscheischler-2018-future-climate-risk-compound-events" />.
