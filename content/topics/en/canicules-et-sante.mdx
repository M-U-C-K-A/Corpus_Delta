Of all climate hazards, heat is the one whose link to warming is most direct and best
established. It is also the one whose health effects are most systematically
underestimated, because they almost never take the form people imagine.

## Heatstroke is not the main mechanism

Deaths labelled directly as heatstroke account for only a small part of the excess mortality
observed during a heat episode. Most of it happens elsewhere: thermal strain aggravates
pre-existing conditions — cardiovascular, respiratory, renal — and the deaths are recorded
under those causes.

This is why heat-related excess mortality is not counted, it is **estimated**, by
statistical comparison with the mortality expected in the same period without a hot spell.

## What international comparison reveals

A multi-country study covering more than 74 million deaths established the curves linking
temperature to mortality in thirteen countries
<Cite id="gasparrini-2015-mortality-risk-attributable-high-low" />. Two results are worth
holding on to.

First, the relationship is U-shaped: mortality rises at both ends of the temperature scale.
Second, and counter-intuitively, it is **moderately** uncomfortable temperatures — not
extremes — that carry the largest share of the total burden, simply because they are far
more frequent.

<Callout variant="info" title="A practical consequence">
Concentrating prevention on alert days alone leaves out a large part of the risk. Structural
measures — insulation, planting, access to cool spaces — act on the whole temperature
distribution, not only on its tail.
</Callout>

## The threshold depends on place, not on a universal value

There is no temperature above which one speaks of a <Term id="canicule">heatwave</Term>.
What counts is the departure from the usual climate: populations, buildings and health
systems are adapted to what they know. A temperature that is ordinary in a Mediterranean
climate is a major health event in a cool temperate one.

<Term id="ilot-de-chaleur-urbain">Urban form</Term> strongly modulates that exposure within
a single city, and the gap with the outskirts is widest at night, precisely when the body
should be recovering.

## Attribution: what can be said about a specific episode

The question "was this heatwave caused by climate change?" is badly posed: every weather
episode results from a combination of factors.
<Term id="attribution">Attribution science</Term> reframes the problem in terms of
probability, comparing the observed world with a simulated world free of human influence.

Heatwaves are the ground on which this method is soundest, because the physical mechanism
linking background warming to extreme temperature is direct.

## Rarely alone

The deadliest episodes often combine several conditions: a preceding drought that dries the
soil and removes evaporative cooling, an absence of wind, ozone pollution favoured by strong
sunlight. Such <Term id="evenement-compose">compound events</Term> are poorly captured by
risk analyses that treat each hazard separately, which systematically underestimate the most
damaging situations <Cite id="zscheischler-2018-future-climate-risk-compound-events" />.

The annual monitoring published by the *Lancet Countdown* documents how these exposures are
evolving worldwide <Cite id="watts-2020-2020-report-lancet-countdown-health" />.
