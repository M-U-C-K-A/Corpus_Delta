Feeding eight billion people takes up a considerable share of land and of the fresh water
withdrawn. The food system therefore occupies a singular position: it ranks among the main
sources of environmental pressure, and among the activities most directly threatened by
climate change.

## Emissions that are not just CO₂

Agriculture emits mainly two gases other than carbon dioxide. **Methane** comes from enteric
fermentation in ruminants and from flooded rice paddies; it has far greater warming power
than CO₂ but a much shorter atmospheric lifetime, which makes accounting for it delicate.
**Nitrous oxide** comes from nitrogen fertilisation and persists, for its part, over a
century.

To these are added the emissions from land conversion: clearing a forest releases the stored
carbon and permanently removes a <Term id="puits-de-carbone">sink</Term>.

## An exposed sector

Yields depend on conditions whose variability is increasing. A
<Term id="canicule">heatwave</Term> falling during flowering can wreck a harvest in a few
days, whatever the season's water balance.

The systemic risk lies less in averages than in
<Term id="evenement-compose">simultaneous events</Term>: concurrent crop failures in several
of the world's breadbaskets would affect markets far more than a localised shortfall, however
severe.

## The double dividend

The EAT-Lancet commission examined diets that satisfy both nutritional needs and
environmental limits
<Cite id="willett-2019-food-anthropocene-eat-lancet-commission" />. Its central conclusion is
that a large share of the changes beneficial for the climate are also beneficial for public
health — which sets this sector apart from many others, where the trade-off is harsher.

<Callout variant="caution" title="An average recommendation is not a prescription">
This work deals in global averages. It says little about any individual diet, and
transposing it depends heavily on agronomic, economic and cultural contexts. The report
itself insists on the point.
</Callout>

The annual *Lancet Countdown* monitoring documents the interactions between climate, food and
health <Cite id="watts-2020-2020-report-lancet-countdown-health" />, within the wider frame of
planetary boundaries, of which land use and biogeochemical cycles form part
<Cite id="steffen-2015-planetary-boundaries-guiding-human-development" />.
