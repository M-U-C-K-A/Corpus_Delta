A city and the countryside around it receive the same sun and the same air. They do not do
the same thing with it. The resulting gap has a name — the
<Term id="ilot-de-chaleur-urbain">urban heat island</Term> — and it is largest when nobody
is watching: at night.

## Four causes, not one

"Concrete stores heat" is true but incomplete. Four mechanisms overlay one another, and
they do not call for the same responses.

The **thermal inertia** of materials: stone, concrete and asphalt accumulate solar energy
by day and release it slowly once night falls. The countryside has long since cooled.

The **absence of evaporation**: vegetated ground spends a large share of the energy it
receives evaporating water, which does not warm the air. An impervious surface has no such
valve, and all the energy goes into sensible heat.

The **geometry**: a narrow street lined with tall buildings forms a cavity. Infrared
radiation escaping towards the sky is intercepted by the facades opposite and returned to
the ground. The apparent <Term id="albedo">albedo</Term> of an urban canyon is lower than
that of its materials taken separately.

The **waste heat**: engines, air conditioners, industrial processes. An air conditioner
does not remove heat, it moves it from inside to the street — which makes it, at
neighbourhood scale, an amplifying loop.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why the gap widens at night">
By day the sun heats everyone and the city-country gap stays modest. By night the
countryside cools by radiation while the city releases its store. The difference is
therefore greatest before dawn, and commonly reaches several degrees.
</Callout>

## Night is precisely the health moment

This chronology is not an observational detail: it explains most of the health risk. A
body absorbs a very hot day if it recovers afterwards. When the night minimum does not fall
below 20 °C — a <Term id="nuit-tropicale">tropical night</Term> — that recovery does not
happen, and thermal load accumulates from one day to the next.

<Cite id="ballester-2023-heat-related-mortality-europe-during" />

The physiological mechanisms run chiefly through the cardiovascular system: to shed heat,
the body raises skin blood flow, which taxes a heart that may already be frail.

<Cite id="liu-2022-heat-exposure-cardiovascular-health-outcomes" />

Humidity weighs as much as temperature. A muggy city at 33 °C can be more dangerous than a
dry one at 40 °C, because sweat no longer evaporates — this is what
<Term id="temperature-humide">wet-bulb temperature</Term> measures.

## What interventions actually deliver

The available synthesis compares interventions tested under real conditions, and the
ranking is sometimes surprising.

**Trees** come first, for two distinct reasons: cast shade, which acts immediately, and
transpiration, which cools the surrounding air. The combined effect exceeds that of a lawn,
which casts no shade.

**Light surfaces** raise albedo and lower surface temperature, sometimes by several
degrees. Their effect on air temperature at head height is more modest, and glare limits
their use in narrow streets.

**Water** — fountains, misting, ponds — acts strongly but over a short radius, and its
consumption raises questions where the resource is already tight.

<Cite id="kumar-2024-urban-heat-mitigation-green-blue" />

<Callout variant="caution" title="An order of magnitude worth keeping in mind">
The best urban interventions return a few degrees. The warming expected by the end of the
century under a high trajectory adds more. Urban adaptation reduces the damage; it does not
substitute for mitigation, and the publications that evaluate it say so without ambiguity.
</Callout>

## An inequality that maps

Exposure is not uniform within a single city. The hottest districts are generally the
densest, the least vegetated and the least served by accessible cool spaces — and they
often house the most vulnerable populations: isolated elderly people, poorly insulated
housing, outdoor work.

This is why a cooling policy is judged as much on its distribution as on its average. A
park added where there already was one shifts little
<Term id="surmortalite">excess mortality</Term>.
