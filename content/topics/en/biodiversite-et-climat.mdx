The most extensively documented effect of climate change on living things is not the
disappearance of species, but their **movement**. It is a quieter phenomenon, with
consequences wider than they appear.

## Following the climate envelope

Every species tolerates a range of conditions. When those conditions shift — polewards,
upslope, deeper for marine species — populations follow, colonising newly favourable margins
and vanishing from those that are no longer so. This movement is observed across all the
major taxonomic groups and on every continent
<Cite id="pecl-2017-biodiversity-redistribution-under-climate-change" />.

## The problem is not the movement, it is its desynchronisation

If every species moved at the same rate, ecosystems would migrate without coming apart. That
is not what happens: speed depends on mobility, on the length of the life cycle, on
dispersal capacity.

The consequences are **decouplings**: a migratory bird arriving after the peak abundance of
the insects it feeds its young on, a pollinator out of step with the flowering it used to
serve, a predator separated from its prey. The disruption strikes ecological relationships
well before it threatens species taken individually.

<Callout variant="info" title="One factor among others, and rarely the first">
For most groups, habitat destruction and fragmentation remain the leading cause of decline
today. Climate acts mainly as an aggravating factor, reducing the capacity of already
weakened populations to absorb one more disturbance.
</Callout>

## At sea, three pressures at once

Marine ecosystems face warming, acidification and
<Term id="desoxygenation-des-oceans">deoxygenation</Term> together
<Cite id="breitburg-2018-declining-oxygen-global-ocean-coastal" />. Fixed organisms —
corals, seagrass, kelp — cannot flee, and a single intense
<Term id="canicule">marine heatwave</Term> is enough to cause mortality from which recovery
takes years.

## A planetary boundary already crossed

The planetary boundaries framework places biosphere integrity among the boundaries whose
transgression is most clearly established — more so than climate itself
<Cite id="steffen-2015-planetary-boundaries-guiding-human-development" />. The two questions
cannot be separated: intact ecosystems are also
<Term id="point-de-bascule">regulators</Term> of the carbon and water cycles.
