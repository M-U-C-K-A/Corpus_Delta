Each year, an international consortium publishes an estimate of every carbon flow between
human activity, the atmosphere, the ocean and the continents. That exercise — the *Global
Carbon Budget* — is one of the few attempts to close a planetary balance completely, and its
method deserves to be understood before its results.

## An equation that has to close

The principle is one of accounting. Carbon emitted by human activity does not disappear: it
ends up in the atmosphere, in the ocean, or in vegetation and soils. The budget consists of
estimating each of those terms independently, then checking that they balance.

Fossil emissions are reconstructed from national energy statistics and cement production.
Atmospheric accumulation, for its part, is the best-known quantity in the whole equation,
because it is measured directly.

## What the Mauna Loa curve measures

The Mauna Loa observatory in Hawaii has produced, since 1958, the longest continuous record
of direct atmospheric CO₂ measurement. Its position — an isolated volcano in the middle of
the Pacific, far from industrial sources — makes it an observation point representative of
background air.

Two things can be read off this curve. The rise is **continuous**: no year shows a decline,
not even those with a global economic slowdown. And it is **accelerating**: the slope of
recent decades is steeper than that of the 1960s.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why economic crises leave no mark">
A temporary fall in emissions slows the rate at which the atmospheric stock grows, but does
not reduce it. As long as emissions stay well above what the
<Term id="puits-de-carbone">sinks</Term> absorb, the concentration keeps climbing.
Flattening the curve means reaching the balance between the two, not cutting emissions a
little.
</Callout>

## The sinks absorb around half of emissions

The ocean and the land biosphere remove a large share of emitted CO₂ from the atmosphere
each year. Without them, the observed accumulation would be roughly twice as fast.

This service is neither free nor guaranteed. Oceanic uptake comes with the acidification of
seawater. Land uptake depends on the health of forests and soils, and so on drought and
fire. And some reservoirs that are inert today, such as
<Term id="permafrost">permafrost</Term>, could switch from sink to source
<Cite id="schuur-2015-climate-change-permafrost-carbon-feedback" />.

## The residual imbalance

The sum of the separately estimated terms never comes out exactly right. That gap — the
*budget imbalance* — is published explicitly rather than distributed arbitrarily across the
other lines.

It is a telling methodological choice: rather than force the equation, the authors display
the size of what they cannot explain
<Cite id="friedlingstein-2023-global-carbon-budget-2023" />. That residual gives an honest
measure of the uncertainty weighing on the least well-constrained flows, particularly those
tied to land-use change.
