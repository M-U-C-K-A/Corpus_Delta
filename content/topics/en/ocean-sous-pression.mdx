The ocean absorbs the great majority of the excess heat accumulated by the climate system,
along with a substantial share of emitted CO₂. This double service has long been described
as a buffer. It has a price, and that price shows up in several transformations best kept
apart.

## Marine heatwaves

The idea of a <Term id="canicule">heatwave</Term> applies to the ocean too: a prolonged spell
of surface temperatures abnormally high for the season and the place. An analysis of the
recent record shows these episodes have become **longer and more frequent** over the past
century <Cite id="oliver-2018-longer-more-frequent-marine-heatwaves" />.

Their effects differ from those of gradual warming. Fixed organisms — corals, seagrass
meadows, kelp forests — cannot flee, and one intense episode is enough to cause mass
mortality from which recovery takes years.

## Deoxygenation

A warmer ocean dissolves less oxygen, and its increased stratification limits exchange
between surface and depth. Near coasts, nutrient inputs from farming and cities add to this,
their decomposition consuming the available oxygen.

The <Term id="desoxygenation-des-oceans">decline in oxygen content</Term> therefore affects
both the open sea and coastal zones, through distinct but converging mechanisms
<Cite id="breitburg-2018-declining-oxygen-global-ocean-coastal" />.

<Callout variant="info" title="Two causes, two levers">
The coastal share of the phenomenon depends directly on farming practices and water
treatment: it can be reduced locally, in the short term. The open-ocean share is tied to
warming itself and will only be corrected along with it.
</Callout>

## The redistribution of life

Faced with conditions that shift, mobile species follow their thermal envelope: towards the
poles, towards the depths. This reorganisation is already extensively documented
<Cite id="pecl-2017-biodiversity-redistribution-under-climate-change" />.

It does not happen in a coordinated way. Species move at different rates depending on their
mobility and life cycle, which pulls apart established ecological relationships — predators
separated from their prey, pollinators out of step with flowering. The consequences spread
beyond ecology: fish stocks cross administrative boundaries that fishing agreements never
anticipated.
