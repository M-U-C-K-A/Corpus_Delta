Producing and using energy is the dominant source of greenhouse gas emissions. It is
therefore where reduction pathways are mainly decided — but the phrase "energy transition"
covers operations of very unequal difficulty.

## Three levers, in this order

**Sufficiency** reduces demand for a service: fewer forced journeys, less leaky buildings,
less intensive uses. It is the cheapest lever technically and the one most dependent on
choices about how things are organised.

**Efficiency** reduces the energy needed for the same service. An electric motor converts a
far greater share of the energy it receives into useful motion than a combustion engine,
which is why electrification cuts primary energy consumption even at unchanged use.

**Substitution** replaces fossil sources with low-carbon ones. It is the most visible lever,
and the one whose feasibility varies most across uses
<Cite id="gielen-2019-role-renewable-energy-global-energy" />.

## Why electrification is the pivot

Decarbonising one carrier — electricity — is a better-posed problem than decarbonising
thousands of end uses separately. Hence the dominant strategy in the scenarios: electrify
what can be electrified, and decarbonise electricity generation.

That leaves the uses which resist direct electrification: steelmaking, cement, heavy
chemicals, long-distance shipping and aviation. These concentrate most of the difficulty
today, and the solutions on the table for them are less mature.

<Callout variant="caution" title="The system, not the source">
Comparing generation options on the cost of the energy they produce alone is misleading.
Variable generation imposes flexibility needs on the rest of the system — storage, grids,
demand management — whose cost belongs on the balance sheet. Conversely, dispatchable
generation has inertia constraints that also carry a price.
</Callout>

## What the scenarios say

Pathways compatible with the Paris Agreement's goals all combine the three levers, in
proportions that vary with the
