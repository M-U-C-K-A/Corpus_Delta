Most consequences of warming are gradual: a little more heat, a little more sea, a little
less ice. A few are not. A tipping point is a threshold beyond which a system moves of its
own accord towards another state, and does not come back when the cause eases.

## What separates a tipping from a worsening

Three properties define the phenomenon, and all three are needed.

There is **self-sustainment**: past the threshold, the transformation continues without the
initial perturbation growing. A <Term id="retroaction-climatique">feedback</Term> that was
damping becomes amplifying.

There is **irreversibility on human timescales**: returning below the threshold does not
return the system to its former state. The outward and return paths do not coincide —
physicists speak of hysteresis. A vanished ice sheet does not reform at the temperature at
which it melted, because once its altitude is lost, its surface sits in warmer air.

And there is a **change of state**, not a mere intensification: a forest becoming savanna,
an ocean circulation changing regime.

<Callout variant="caution" title="What 'irreversible' means here">
On geological timescales almost everything reforms. The irreversibility at issue is
counted in centuries or millennia, that is, far beyond any conceivable public policy. For a
society, the distinction from "permanent" has no practical bearing.
</Callout>

## The inventory, and what it is worth

The reference survey identifies sixteen elements liable to tip, nine of them planetary in
reach. For each it proposes a triggering temperature range, and it is on those ranges
rather than on the central values that attention belongs.

<Cite id="mckay-2022-exceeding-global-warming-could-trigger" />

Four elements are given as possibly crossable within the current warming range: the
Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, warm-water coral reefs, and some
<Term id="permafrost">permafrost</Term> regions. The ranges remain wide and confidence
uneven between elements.

The Amazon case is distinctive because the threshold there is not only climatic. The forest
recycles its own rain: part of the moisture falling in the western basin has already been
evaporated several times by the canopy upstream. Deforestation and drought therefore attack
the same mechanism from two sides.

<Cite id="flores-2024-critical-transitions-amazon-forest-system" />

## The signs that can be observed

A system approaching a tipping does not announce it clearly, but it leaves statistical
traces. It takes longer to return to equilibrium after a perturbation — this is **critical
slowing down** — and its variability rises. Such traces are sought in long observational
records, and their interpretation remains debated: slowing can have other causes.

The Atlantic meridional overturning circulation is the most studied case. Work finds a
weakening relative to the past millennium, reconstructed from indirect indicators for want
of a direct measurement long enough.

<Cite id="caesar-2018-observed-fingerprint-weakening-atlantic-ocean" />

Permafrost illustrates a neighbouring difficulty: the Arctic carbon balance depends largely
on what happens in winter, a season long under-instrumented. Winter measurements show CO₂
losses above what models assumed.

<Cite id="natali-2019-large-loss-co2-winter-observed" />

## Why uncertainty is not reassuring

The common objection is that these thresholds are poorly known, therefore speculative. The
reasoning reverses.

A wide range means the threshold may lie **lower** as readily as higher. Facing irreversible
damage, the asymmetry is total: erring by overestimating the threshold costs mitigation
spending; erring by underestimating it costs a state of the system that no later spending
buys back.

Added to this is the possibility of **cascades** — one tipping triggering another. The
survey identifies plausible couplings, but the literature quantifies them poorly, and that
gap is itself a matter of the unknown rather than the reassuring.

<Cite id="kemp-2022-climate-endgame-exploring-catastrophic-climate" />

That is the underlying argument: the best-studied scenarios are the central trajectories,
not the tails of the distribution. Yet it is the tails that carry the risk one cannot
insure against.
