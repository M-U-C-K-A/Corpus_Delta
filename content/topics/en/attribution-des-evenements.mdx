After every heatwave, every flood, the same question returns: was this climate change?
Put that way, it has no answer. Put differently, it has one, and increasingly quickly.

## The question that cannot be asked

A weather event always has an immediate cause: a blocking high, a surge of tropical air, a
stalled depression. Climate does not create these configurations; it changes how often they
arise and how intense they become when they do.

Asking whether "warming caused" a heatwave is therefore like asking whether a loaded die
caused the six just thrown. The six might have come up anyway. What can be established is
how heavily the die is loaded.

<Callout variant="info" title="The reframing that makes the question tractable">
The question is not "was this event due to climate?" but "in a world without warming, how
probable would such an event have been, and how probable is it in the real world?" The
ratio between the two is the study's result.
</Callout>

## How it is done

The dominant method compares two simulated worlds. The first is the world as it is, with
its observed atmospheric composition. The second is a counterfactual world, identical but
with human emissions removed. Hundreds of simulations of each are run, then one counts in
how many of them the studied event, or worse, occurs.

The result is expressed two ways. The **probability ratio** says how much more likely the
event has become: "ten times more likely" means it used to occur once a century and now
occurs once a decade. The **intensity change** says how much worse it has become: "2 °C
hotter" for a heatwave of the same <Term id="periode-de-retour">return period</Term>.

<Cite id="fischer-2015-anthropogenic-contribution-global-occurrence-heavy" />

The two framings are not interchangeable. An event may be barely more likely yet markedly
more intense, or the reverse. Coverage generally keeps whichever figure is the more
striking.

## What attributes well, and what resists

Not all categories of event lend themselves equally to the exercise. Heat attributes best:
the signal is strong, the mechanism direct, and models represent it well. Most recent major
heatwaves turn out, on analysis, to be near-impossible without warming.

Extreme precipitation comes next, with a solid physical basis — warmer air holds more water
vapour — but local variability that demands long records. Droughts are harder, because they
combine rainfall, <Term id="evapotranspiration">evapotranspiration</Term> and human water
use. Tropical cyclones resist most: they are rare, intense, and poorly resolved by
coarse-grid models.

<Cite id="bartusek-2022-2021-north-american-heatwave-amplified" />

One case deserves particular attention. Some recent events have exceeded not only observed
records but what simulations judged possible — the Pacific Northwest heat dome of June 2021
is the example. Such episodes pose a different question from ordinary attribution: not "how
much has the risk risen?" but "do our models bound the possible correctly?"

## From probability to damage

A second generation of work extends the chain to consequences. If one knows how much a
heatwave was worsened, and knows the relationship between temperature and mortality, one
can estimate how many deaths are attributable to the human share of the warming.

<Cite id="mitchell-2016-attributing-human-mortality-during-extreme" />

The same reasoning applies to economic costs. These estimates chain more assumptions than
physical attribution alone, and their uncertainty is wider — which the publications state,
and which coverage almost always omits.

<Cite id="newman-2023-global-costs-extreme-weather-that" />

## Reading an attribution result

Three precautions are enough to avoid the commonest misreadings.

**A probability ratio is not a percentage of responsibility.** "Ten times more likely" does
not mean "90 % caused by climate": the event remains meteorological in form, and
<Term id="variabilite-interne">internal variability</Term> keeps its share in it.

**No detected signal is not no effect.** For rare or poorly observed hazards, a study may
conclude that it cannot decide. That is a statement about data quality, not about climate.

**Speed has a price.** Analyses published within days of an event use streamlined methods
and models already to hand. They serve public debate and are generally confirmed later, but
their range is wider than a peer-reviewed study's — and the
<Term id="niveau-de-confiance">confidence level</Term> they state says so.
