What does a tonne of CO₂ cost? The question looks technical; it is the most value-laden in
the whole climate file. Published answers span more than an order of magnitude, and the
spread does not come from calculation errors.

## Three costs not to be conflated

Public debate routinely mixes three distinct quantities.

The **cost of damages** is what warming destroys: lost harvests, damaged buildings,
unworkable days, shortened lives. It is suffered.

The **cost of mitigation** is what cutting emissions costs: investment, retraining, assets
abandoned before amortisation. It is incurred deliberately.

The **social cost of carbon** is an estimate of the first, brought down to the tonne: the
discounted value of all future damage caused by a tonne emitted today. It is an object of
calculation, not an observation, and it is where the disagreements concentrate.

<Callout variant="info" title="One figure, three implicit decisions">
Every estimate of the social cost of carbon assumes a discount rate, a damage function and
a rule for aggregating across rich and poor countries. All three choices are normative.
Changing the discount rate alone is enough to multiply the result fivefold.
</Callout>

## The discount rate, or the weight of the future

Discounting brings a future damage back to a present value. A rate of 5 % reduces a damage
occurring a century hence to under a hundredth of its value; a rate of 1.5 % leaves it at
nearly a quarter.

Two justifications are opposed. The **descriptive** approach takes rates observed in
markets, on the grounds that they reflect real trade-offs. The **prescriptive** approach
objects that a market rate expresses living agents' preference for their own consumption,
and that there is no ethical reason to apply the same discount to generations with no say.

No statistic settles this. It is wholly normative, and an estimate that does not publish
its rate cannot be interpreted.

## Health damages, long under-counted

Classical damage functions aggregated losses in points of GDP, which captures mortality
poorly. More recent work isolates that component and converts it into deaths attributable
to a given quantity of emissions.

<Cite id="bressler-2021-mortality-cost-carbon" />

The exercise remains sensitive to the value placed on a statistical life, another
normative parameter, but it shifts the result: the health component turns out to be of the
same order as the entire economic damage previously counted.

On the side of damages already incurred, attribution now allows the share of an extreme
event's costs imputable to warming to be quantified.

## Risk as finance sees it

The financial sector uses a different split, into two families. **Physical risk** is that
of damages suffered: destroyed assets, interrupted supply chains. **Transition risk** is
what a rapid decarbonisation would create for the assets that depend on fossil carbon —
reserves rendered unexploitable, industries whose model rests on carbon being free.

The two move in opposite directions along a trajectory: vigorous action lowers the first
and raises the second. No trajectory cancels both, which is why a banking supervisor
cannot work from a single scenario.

## What policy evaluation shows

Beyond prospective estimates, a retrospective literature examines what schemes already in
force have produced. The synthesis of *ex post* evaluations of carbon pricing finds real
but generally modest reductions, strongly dependent on the level of the
<Term id="prix-du-carbone">price</Term> and on the breadth of exemptions.

<Cite id="dobbeling-2024-systematic-review-meta-analysis-post" />

This is a useful result because it bears on observed facts rather than projections. It
does not say the instrument fails; it says its effect is proportionate to its ambition,
which is unremarkable and yet rarely absorbed into debates about its principle.

## Reading a cost figure

Three questions are enough to place an estimate. What **discount rate**? Which **damage
components** are included, and which are absent for want of being quantifiable —
ecosystems, migration, conflict? And does the estimate aggregate losses **weighted by
income**, in which case a death in a poor country mechanically counts for less than a
death in a rich one?

An estimate that answers these three questions is usable even if one disputes its choices.
One that does not is not.
