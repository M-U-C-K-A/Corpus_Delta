## A logarithmic scale, and not the one you think

Magnitude characterises the earthquake itself: a tremor has only one magnitude, wherever it
is observed from. That is what distinguishes it from
<Term id="intensite-macrosismique">macroseismic intensity</Term>, which describes the
effects felt at a given place and therefore varies from one town to the next.

The scale is **logarithmic**: one extra point of magnitude corresponds to roughly a tenfold
increase in recorded signal amplitude, and about thirtyfold in energy released. The gap
between magnitude 6 and magnitude 8 is therefore not a factor of two, but several thousand.

<Callout variant="caution" title="Calling it the Richter scale is a misnomer">
The scale Charles Richter proposed in 1935 was calibrated for local Californian earthquakes
and saturates for major events. Monitoring agencies now use moment magnitude, which is
truer to large tremors while keeping a comparable graduation. The word "Richter" survives
mostly in the press.
</Callout>
