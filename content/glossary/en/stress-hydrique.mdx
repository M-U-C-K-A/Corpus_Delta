## A matter of demand as much as rainfall

Water stress does not measure drought. It sets what human uses withdraw against what the
water cycle renews. A well-watered but heavily used region can be under stress; an arid, sparsely
populated one may not be.

The distinction from <Term id="secheresse">drought</Term> is therefore sharp: drought is a temporary
deficit against the local normal, stress a structural imbalance between supply and demand. The two
compound — a drought bites harder where the resource was already tight — but they call for different
levers: one crisis management, the other a revision of uses.

Climate acts on both sides of the ratio. It alters precipitation and
<Term id="evapotranspiration">evapotranspiration</Term>, hence supply; and it raises agricultural
demand, since a crop under drier air needs more irrigation for the same yield. Groundwater
depletion, often invisible, is the most lasting symptom of that imbalance.
