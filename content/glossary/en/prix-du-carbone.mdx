## Putting a cost on what had none

Emitting CO₂ imposes a cost on third parties — health, harvests, coastlines — that the
emitter does not pay. Economists call this an externality. Carbon pricing gives it a price, through
a **tax** that fixes the cost and lets quantity float, or a <Term id="marche-carbone">cap-and-trade
market</Term> that fixes quantity and lets price settle.

Two distinct quantities share the name and are often conflated. The **observed price** is what
emitters actually pay where a scheme exists; it covers roughly a quarter of global emissions and
remains, for the most part, far below the second quantity. The **social cost of carbon** is an
estimate of the damage an additional tonne causes, and belongs to economic calculation, not
observation.

Retrospective evaluations of existing schemes find real but generally modest reductions, strongly
dependent on the price level and on the breadth of exemptions.
