## Little area, large stock

Mangroves, seagrass meadows and salt marshes cover a minute fraction of the planet's
vegetated surface. Yet they store carbon per hectare at a rate well above that of a tropical forest.

The reason lies not in the plant but in the soil. Coastal sediments are waterlogged and therefore
oxygen-poor, which sharply slows the decomposition of organic matter. Carbon accumulates there over
millennia instead of being recycled within decades, and the sediment layer keeps thickening as
<Term id="niveau-marin">sea level</Term> rises.

That strength is also their fragility. Draining a mangrove or turning over a marsh returns the stock
to contact with oxygen and releases within a few years carbon accumulated over centuries. Destroying
these habitats therefore turns them into sources, and conserving them protects an existing stock
rather than creating a new one — which makes them a real but area-limited
<Term id="attenuation">mitigation</Term> lever.
