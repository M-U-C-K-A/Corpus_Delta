## The gain taken back with one hand

Making a use more efficient makes it cheaper, and what costs less gets used more. An
engine that burns less per kilometre lowers the price of a kilometre; part of the fuel saved goes
back into extra kilometres. That is the rebound effect.

It is distinguished at three scales. **Direct** rebound applies to the use itself: a better-insulated
house gets heated a little warmer. **Indirect** rebound applies to the freed spending, redirected to
other consumption. **Macroeconomic** rebound follows from cheaper energy making activities viable
that previously were not.

Estimates vary widely by sector and income level, but the effect is almost always partial: it bites
into the gain without cancelling it. Its practical import lies elsewhere — it explains why a policy
resting on efficiency alone delivers less than its engineering calculation suggests, and why it is
often paired with a price signal or a quantity cap.
