## Why humidity changes everything

The human body sheds heat by sweating: water evaporating from the skin carries energy
away. That release depends not on temperature alone but on how much more vapour the air can
accept. In already saturated air, sweat stops evaporating and the mechanism fails.

Wet-bulb temperature measures precisely this. It is obtained by wrapping a thermometer's bulb in
a ventilated wet cloth: evaporation cools the instrument the more, the drier the air. In dry air
it sits far below the ordinary temperature; in saturated air the two coincide.

Above roughly **35 °C wet-bulb**, a human body at rest, in shade and properly hydrated can no
longer cool itself, whatever its fitness. The threshold is theoretical, and recent work places it
lower under real conditions, around 30 to 31 °C. It explains why 40 °C in dry air and 33 °C in
muggy air are not remotely the same danger — and why a <Term id="canicule">heatwave</Term> is never
judged on the thermometer alone.
