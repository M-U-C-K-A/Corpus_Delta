## Two opposite effects in the same object

A cloud reflects part of the incoming sunlight back to space, which cools. And it absorbs
the infrared radiation emitted by the surface, which warms. Which of the two prevails
depends on the cloud's altitude, its thickness and the size of its droplets.

The low, thick clouds over the oceans cool markedly. Cirrus, thin and high, warm. The
question is therefore not whether clouds cool, but how their distribution will shift in a
warmer climate.

<Callout variant="uncertainty" title="The models' weak point">
A cloud forms at scales far below a climate model's grid cell: it has to be represented
indirectly, through approximations. That is where most of the spread between models comes
from <Cite id="eyring-2016-overview-coupled-model-intercomparison-project" />, and hence the
width of the <Term id="sensibilite-climatique">sensitivity</Term> range.
</Callout>
